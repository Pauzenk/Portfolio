JARVIS²: operator interface for an AI agent that runs your ERP

CHAT

Desktop frame, dark UI
01 What it is

Most business data sits in systems with no API: 1C, old SAP, internal portals. Classic RPA breaks the moment a button moves. JARVIS² takes another route: it looks at the screen like a human operator, recognises forms visually, and clicks. It runs locally on a normal office PC without a GPU, and nothing leaves the company perimeter.

I did the UX/UI audit and built the interactive prototype of the operator interface. The prototype was used in the pitch to prospective pilot clients.

02 The design problem

An agent that acts on a live accounting database is not a chatbot. The user has to see what it is about to do, stop it, and understand what happened after. That is the whole interface.

The first version told the user everything except the things that mattered. Every one of the eight steps got its own chat message, so the screen read as a log; the same tap that opened a document also sent an invoice to a counterparty, with no distinction between the two; and the reports screen led with a success rate, a streak counter and a health bar for the agent. What it never showed was which database the agent was working in, how autonomous it currently was, or what had been rolled back after a failure. The redesign moved all of that to the surface and pushed the celebration out.

03 Confirmation before irreversible steps

The reference scenario ends with “show me before sending”. An agent that pulls June sales from 1C, builds a report and emails the director must stop at the last step and wait.

The irreversible step gets its own card, marked IRREVERSIBLE, and it carries everything needed to say yes without opening 1C. The recipient and the amount at the top. Then "Checked before sending — 3 of 3 matched": the amount reconciled against the document lines, the counterparty against contract #45 with its tax ID, the document status read off the 1C screen — each check with the value it found, so the operator is verifying data, not trusting a sentence. Below that, in red, the consequence in plain words: the document goes to the counterparty and the agent will not be able to recall it. Confirmation is a 1.5-second hold, not a click — the fill has to reach the end, so a reflex tap cannot send an invoice.

04 Autonomy that only a human can raise

The agent lowers its own autonomy when the interface behaves unexpectedly, but it can never restore it by itself. Only the operator can. So the level has to be visible at all times, and the reason for a drop has to be readable, not buried in logs.

The level sits in the header at all times — AUTONOMY L1, asks on every step, lowered by the agent — so the operator never has to go looking for it. When the agent drops the level, it says so in the thread as a marked event: L2 → L1, what went wrong (the posting dialog opened twice at step 5), and what it will do until that is resolved. Raising it back is a human action: Restore level L2.

05 When a step fails

The system rolls back to a checkpoint: a failure at step 8 of 10 undoes what steps 1 to 7 wrote into 1C and the file system. Technically solid, and invisible unless the interface says so. The user needs one screen answering: what broke, what was undone, what is still there, what now.

06 What I'd do differently

I designed screens before I understood which states actually matter. The confirmation card looks convincing because all three checks pass. The interesting state is two out of three: something did not match, and the accountant has to decide anyway. Next time I would start from the states where the system is unsure, not from the happy path.

Three users, one water network

Cover

Redesigning SUEZ's water-monitoring platforms — from the internal supervision tool to the client dashboards — turning a tangled legacy system into ergonomic, role-appropriate interfaces.

01 Context

SUEZ operates smart water meters across France — thousands of connected devices reporting consumption, leaks and technical faults. The data served three very different audiences: internal operations teams supervising the meter fleet, professional clients managing multi-site consumption, and individual households watching a single meter.

The existing system had grown into a hard-to-navigate legacy: dense, inconsistent, and built around the data rather than the people reading it. An operator hunting for a faulty meter, a facility manager comparing sites, and a homeowner worried about a leak were all served the same tangled logic.

02 The brief

Rework the user journeys and make the system genuinely usable — a more ergonomic platform for water monitoring, built on SUEZ's existing identity guidelines. Not a visual restyling: a structural redesign of how each audience finds, reads and acts on water data.

03 The approach: one system, three levels of expertise

The core design decision was to treat the three audiences as three distinct products sharing one design language — rather than one interface with permissions.

04 Supervision

Built for operators who live in the tool

A cartographic view of the meter fleet with color-coded statuses, per-emitter detail sheets, alarm synthesis (leaks, water returns, frozen meters, battery, connectivity), and diagnostic lists with bulk actions — an operator can select dozens of emitters and mark them as treated in one confirmed action. Density is a feature here, not a bug: the design optimizes for scanning thousands of devices.

05 Espace professionnel

Built for accountability across sites

Multi-site clients get a dashboard of consumption by meter and by usage, a per-meter synthesis table with trends, and configurable alerts (leak / overconsumption) with thresholds and notification channels. The design assumes a professional reading in m³, comparing sites and reporting to someone.

06 Espace particulier

Built for a homeowner who checks once a week

The same data, radically simplified: daily consumption in liters, a comparison gauge against similar households, one meter, plain-language questions to profile the home, and the same leak alerts — because a night-time continuous flow means something very different when it's your house.

One UI kit, three densities.

07 Key design decisions

Ergonomics over exhaustiveness. The legacy showed everything everywhere. The redesign gives each journey a hierarchy: what needs action first (alarms, thresholds crossed), then what explains it (curves, history), then everything else behind a click.

Shared patterns, different densities. Alerts, thresholds and consumption charts use the same components across the three spaces — but the internal tool packs them tight for scanning, while the household space spends the same patterns generously. One UI kit, three densities.

Designed inside an existing identity. SUEZ's brand guidelines were a given. The design system work focused on what identity guides don't cover: data visualization rules, status color semantics, table and alert patterns, threshold form conventions.

Design through development. Clickable prototypes served as the specification, and the design was followed through implementation with the two developers — decisions were adjusted against technical reality rather than thrown over the wall.

08 Outcome

The redesigned journeys shipped with the development team during the engagement. The strongest evidence of the work is structural: a legacy system that required expert knowledge to navigate became three role-appropriate products — an operations tool an agent can scan, a professional dashboard a facility manager can report from, and a household space a non-technical person can understand at a glance — all within one design language and one UI kit.

What this project demonstrates

redesigning large functional areas of a complex, data-heavy B2B system — information architecture, interaction patterns and a scalable UI kit — while collaborating tightly with product and engineering through delivery.

UBITIK · iOS & Android · white-label B2B2C

End-to-end product design of a B2B2C mobile app connecting families building a home with their constructeur — designed from a brief in 2020, still live and adopted by home builders across France.

01 Context

Building an individual home in France takes 12 to 18 months. For the family, most of that time is a black box: the builder has the information, the client has the anxiety. Communication happens through scattered emails, phone calls to the office, and paper documents that get lost somewhere between the compromis and the remise des clés.

For builders, this is expensive too — every worried client is another call to handle, another email thread to dig through, another document to resend.

02 The brief

Design a mobile app that becomes the single channel between the client and their builder — not just during construction, but across the entire lifecycle of the home: from signing the contract to warranty claims years after delivery. The product had to work as a white-label platform: one design, many builders, each presenting it to their clients as their own service.

03 The approach: design around the life of the home, not around features

The key structural decision was to organize the product around the home's lifecycle rather than a feature list. Each phase of the client's journey maps to a dedicated space in the app.

04 During construction

Visibility over anxiety

A typed activity feed (documents, e-signatures, site photos, completed milestones) so the client always knows what happened and what's next. A structured roadmap of the build — VRD, finitions, livraison, raccordement — that turns an opaque industrial process into a readable progress map. And deadline-driven reminders for the client's own obligations (open the water meter, contact the mairie), because delays are often on the client side: the app manages both directions of responsibility.

05 At delivery

The snag list, structured

Defect reporting with photos, intervention deadlines, status tracking (en cours / non traité / résolu) and a message thread per issue — replacing the classic paper snag list.

06 Living in the home

Useful after the sale

A library of manuals for installed equipment, and seasonal maintenance advice with per-task notification opt-ins.

One design, many builders — presented as their own service.

07 Key design decisions

A typed event feed instead of a chat. Communication between client and builder is heterogeneous: documents to sign, photos, milestones, requests with deadlines. A generic chat would flatten all of it. The feed types every event and gives each type its own affordances — sign, download, reply, validate — keeping the history scannable months later.

Deadlines as first-class objects. Construction runs on dates. Every request, reserve and reminder carries a visible deadline with consistent treatment across the app — one visual language for urgency everywhere.

Designed for the least technical user. The buyer persona spans from young couples to retirees building their last home. Large tap targets, one action per screen, bottom-sheet confirmations for every destructive action, and plain-language French throughout.

White-label by structure. Builder identity lives in a contained set of surfaces — branding, contacts, step templates — while the core flows stay identical, allowing the platform to scale across builders without per-client redesign. The shipped product runs in nine languages.

08 Outcome

The app shipped and is still live today — version 5.3.1, updated as recently as December 2024, years after design delivery. It has been adopted by home builders across France, who present it on their own websites as part of their client experience: Maisons et Chalets des Alpes offers it as a lifetime espace client “from the start of the worksite until after delivery”; Les Pavillons Montois positions it as their real-time construction tracking interface; MTC Maisons describes it as “a real communication tool that strengthens our relationship throughout your project”.

An App Store review captured the product's reason to exist better than any pitch: “Finally, someone thought about tracking the construction of new homes!”

What this project demonstrates

designing a complete product system from a brief — information architecture, interaction patterns and content design — robust enough to survive years of evolution and scale as a multi-tenant platform.

01 What it is

You type what you ate the way you would tell a friend. The AI turns that into calories and macros, shows its guess, and waits. You fix anything that is off, then log it.

The whole product was built by directing an AI coding agent against strict specs, with the design decisions kept on my side of the table. It is live now as a progressive web app with 40+ users.

02 The estimate card

Every estimate carries its assumption in plain sight. “1 medium, ~110g” is not hidden in a tooltip. It sits under the number, and tapping it opens the edit before anything is saved. Calories and macros are both editable there.

After logging, the card confirms exactly what changed — the meal it went to and the calorie delta, like “Added to Lunch · +350 kcal” — with a five-second undo beside it. Correcting a guess should cost less than making one.

The number is never the whole answer. The assumption is.

03 Guardrails

Goals that fall out of a safe range get an inline warning: a floor at 1,200 kcal for loss, and a mirrored cap for gain mode.

The warning explains, it does not block. People know things about their own bodies that the app does not, so the app says what it sees and leaves the decision where it belongs.

04 Designing trust in AI actions

Three rules shaped every AI-driven action in the product. They are simple enough to check any screen against.

1

Show assumptions

If the model guessed a portion, a weight or a preparation, that guess is stated explicitly next to the result.

2

Make outputs editable

Every calorie and macro field the AI produced is tap-to-edit in place, before it becomes data.

3

Make actions reversible

Logging, deleting, adjusting: each has a five-second undo within reach.

Show it. Let me change it. Let me take it back.

05 Working with an AI agent as design material

I did not write the code. I wrote the specs, tightly, and directed a coding agent to build against them. The specs were where design happened: states, edge cases, copy, what a warning may and may not do.

The prompts were the real deliverable. Four habits kept them honest.

Input
Strict written specs
Builder
AI coding agent
Review
Designer, every change
Output
Live PWA, 40+ users
01

Constraints first

Say what the agent must avoid before what it should do. Boundaries, then features.

02

Audit before fix

Ask for a report on what is wrong first. Reporting and implementation stay separate steps.

03

Verify the render

Check the built screen, not the agent's account of it. Claimed fixes are not fixes.

04

Kill duplicated logic

One rule, one place. Duplicated logic across functions gets collapsed on sight.

06 What's next

The consumer app is the way in. On its own it is a nutrition tracker; the point is what it makes possible next.

That next thing is a dashboard for nutritionists: manage clients, view their logs, track adherence, and adjust plans that push back to the app. B2B2C, with the consumer PWA as the distribution channel.

In hindsight — define the calculation model before any UI.

01 What it is

Open Feedback is a platform for people who check facts for a living — journalists, scientists, fact-checkers — to analyse credibility data at scale and tell the difference between truth and misinformation.

I created the product and led the tech team to launch it: design strategy, team collaboration, and delivery.

02 The problem

At the time, no dedicated tool existed. Experts were verifying information by hand, across fragmented sources, with nothing holding it together.

The job was to turn that into a structured system: identify reliable sources, surface misinformation, and support the decision at the end of it.

No tool existed. Experts checked every source by hand.

03 My role

I worked as Product Designer and Product Owner, leading the product from concept to delivery — design on one side of the table, backlog and delivery on the other.

04 The interface

Three main surfaces: the Search and Media pages for finding and reading sources, and an Editors Dashboard for the people managing the analysis.

The hard part was the data itself. Credibility is many criteria at once, so the screens had to make a dense dataset legible at a glance and drillable when it mattered.

Credibility data, structured to be read.

05 Design system & documentation

The interface ran on a proper system: a type scale with defined roles, spacing tokens, a fixed set of breakpoints, and a component library the developers built against.

All of it lived in Notion as a single shared reference, so design decisions and their rationale stayed next to the people implementing them.

As Product Owner I ran user stories and objectives, and kept a Kanban board that design and development shared.

On the data side, I worked with the backend on the data flow — diagram, schema, and the data model — so the structure behind the screens matched what the analysis actually needed.
